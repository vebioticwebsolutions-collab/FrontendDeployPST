What Actually Makes a Walkway Plank Anti-Skid?

Every supplier says their plank is anti-skid. Very few can tell you what that actually means, or how to check it. Here is the plain-English explanation, with the real numbers behind it.

Scaffold Safety Guide · 5 min read · Prime SteelTech

A worker steps onto a plank after a light drizzle. On one plank, his boot grips instantly. On another, it slides half an inch before catching. Both planks were sold as "anti-skid." Only one of them actually is.

Anti-Skid Is Not a Marketing Word

"Anti-skid" gets printed on almost every scaffold plank quote in India, but it is not a yes or no feature. Real anti-skid grip depends on three things: how many grip points the surface has, how those grip points are shaped, and how well water drains away underfoot. A plank can look anti-skid in a photo and still fail on site. The difference comes down to the dimples: how many there are, how they are spaced, and how they are made.

What Is a Dimple, Actually?

A dimple is a small dome pressed into the steel sheet, along with a tiny drain hole next to it. It is formed by pressing the steel itself into shape while it is still flat sheet, before the plank is folded into its final profile. It is not paint or a coating, so it cannot wear off the way a coating can. As long as the sheet has not corroded or been ground down, the dimple stays exactly where it was formed.

A flat, painted anti-skid coating only does the first job. It gives some texture, but water still pools on top of it. A pressed dimple with a drain hole does both jobs together, and that combination is what actually keeps a boot from sliding.

The Number That Actually Matters: Dimple Density

Anyone can press a few dimples onto a sheet and call it anti-skid. What separates a genuinely grippy plank from a token one is dimple density: how many dimples are pressed into every square metre of deck. Prime SteelTech's Anti-Skid Walkway Plank is built to a density of 246.11 dimples per square metre, one of the higher densities available on the Indian market for this product.

In plain terms, no point on the deck is ever far from a grip point. Wherever a boot lands, it meets a raised dome within a few centimetres, in any direction. That is what "omnidirectional grip" actually means. It is the direct result of even, close spacing between dimples in both directions.

Contrast this with a low-density plank. If dimples are spaced far apart, there are flat patches of smooth steel between them, exactly where a boot slides. There is no single legal minimum dimple count enforced across every seller, so a supplier can technically call almost any plank anti-skid. That is exactly why the density number matters more than the word on the invoice.

Not All Anti-Skid Patterns Are the Same

Dimples are the standard anti-skid surface on Prime SteelTech planks, but they are not the only pattern used in the industry. It helps to know the difference, since some sites specify a different pattern for a reason.

Pattern How it grips Best suited for
Antiskid Dimple Plate Hemispherical domes with drain perforations, omnidirectional grip General scaffold access and working platforms, standard on all Prime SteelTech planks
Diamond Checker Plate Embossed diamond pattern, grips well in both directions of travel Scaffold and access platforms where boots move in straight lines rather than turning
Checker Plate Parallel raised ribs with drainage channels running between them Heavy-duty industrial platforms, oil and gas plants, food-processing walkways

For a straightforward scaffold access route with people walking in every direction, the dimple plate is almost always the right call. It is also the pattern most tested for all-direction grip, which is why it is the standard fit rather than an upgrade.

How the Dimples Handle Water

Grip alone does not solve the slip problem. If water sits on top of a textured surface, the water itself becomes the slippery layer, no matter how good the texture is underneath. This is where the perforations next to each dimple come in.

A plank with only a raised texture and no drainage holes performs worse in real monsoon conditions than the numbers suggest. On paper, both planks look "anti-skid." On a wet scaffold at seven in the morning, only the one with working drainage keeps its grip.

What Ruins Anti-Skid Grip Over Time

Anti-skid dimples do not last forever if they are treated carelessly. Three things degrade them faster than normal wear:

  • Painting over the deck. Paint fills in the dimples and blocks the drain holes. Touch-up paint is meant for cut edges and weld zones only, never the walking surface.
  • Corrosion eating into the sheet. If the zinc coating wears through below its minimum of 45 microns, rust starts to eat into the dimples, rounding off the domes and softening the grip.
  • Heavy abrasive traffic. Constant dragging of steel tools, pipes, or trolleys across the same patch flattens dimples faster than foot traffic alone.

Under IS 3696, a plank should be removed from active use if it shows buckling, holes, or more than 20 percent loss of its anti-skid pattern. Below that threshold, enough of the original dimple grid is still in place to keep the grip roughly even across the deck. Above it, you start getting the same smooth patches that make a low-density plank dangerous in the first place.

A dimple you cannot see is a dimple that is not working. If a plank looks smoother or shinier than it used to, that is not cosmetic wear. That is grip disappearing.

Quick Answers

What does dimple density mean on a plank datasheet?

It is the number of raised anti-skid domes pressed into every square metre of deck. Prime SteelTech planks are built to 246.11 dimples per square metre, spaced 65 mm apart vertically and 50 mm apart horizontally.

Is a checker plate the same as a dimple plate?

No. A checker plate uses raised parallel ribs, while a dimple plate uses individual raised domes with drain holes between them. Dimple plates grip well in every direction, which is why they are the standard fit for general scaffold access.

Can you paint over anti-skid dimples to touch up a plank?

No. Painting over the dimples fills the raised texture and blocks the drainage holes, removing both the grip and the drainage the dimples are designed for. Touch-up paint should only go on cut edges and weld zones, never the walking surface.

When should a plank be taken out of service for worn anti-skid grip?

Under IS 3696, a plank should be removed from service if more than 20 percent of its anti-skid pattern has worn away, or if the deck shows buckling or holes. A quick visual check before each use is enough to catch this early.

The Short Version

Anti-skid grip on a GI walkway plank comes from three things working together: enough dimples, spaced closely and evenly in both directions, each one paired with a drain hole so water never gets the chance to pool. A plank that skips any one of these can still be labelled anti-skid. It will not behave like one on a wet morning.
