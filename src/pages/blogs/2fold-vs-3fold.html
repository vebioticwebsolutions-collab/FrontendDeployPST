The Simple Guide to Walkway Planks: 2-Fold vs 3-Fold

By Dhruv Rohira | 6 Min Read

When you look at a tall building being built, you see a metal frame on the outside. This frame is called scaffolding. Men and women work high up on this frame. To walk safely, they step on long steel boards. These steel boards are called Walkway Planks.

Walking 50 floors up in the air is scary. The board you walk on must be very strong. It must not break. It must not bend too much. And it must not be slippery.

Today, we will learn how these steel boards are made to keep workers safe. We will look at a very important part of the board: the "fold." We will learn the difference between a 2-fold plank and a 3-fold plank in very simple English.

Think of a walkway plank as a bridge. The stronger the bridge, the safer you feel. Folds in the steel are the secret to making this bridge strong.

What is a Walkway Plank?

A walkway plank is a long flat piece of metal. But it is not just any metal. Good planks, like the ones made by Prime Steeltech, use high-quality steel (called IS 2062 E250 steel). The steel sheet is usually 2 mm thick (14 Gauge) or 1.6 mm thick (16 Gauge).

If you leave normal steel outside in the rain, it gets rust. Rust makes the steel weak. So, the steel is pre-galvanized. This means it is covered in a layer of zinc. The zinc layer must be at least 45 micrometers thick (we write this as 45 µm). This zinc acts like a raincoat. It stops the rust.

Stopping the Slip

When workers are high up, they cannot slip. The plank has a special top surface called a deck. The deck has small bumps called "dimples." These dimples stick up to grab the worker's shoes. Between the dimples, there are holes. If it rains, the water falls through the holes. Dirt and small stones also fall through the holes. A safe board has about 246 dimples per square meter. This gives an omnidirectional grip, meaning your shoe will not slip in any direction.

The Big Problem: Deflection (Bending)

If you put a piece of wood between two chairs and stand on it in the middle, the wood bends down. In construction, this bending is called deflection.

A little bending is normal. But too much bending is very bad. The global safety rules (like IS 3696 from India and OSHA from America) say the plank must not bend more than a specific limit. This limit is the length of the span divided by 60. So, if the supports are 2000 mm apart, the plank must not bend down more than 33 mm. It should feel stiff and solid.

How do we stop a flat piece of steel from bending? We use stiffeners and folds.

Stiffeners

Under the plank, extra pieces of steel are welded on. These are called transverse stiffeners. They are placed every 500 mm. They cross the width of the plank and act like small bones, holding the plank straight.

What is a Fold?

Take a piece of paper. Hold it at the ends. It is very floppy, right? Now, fold the long edges of the paper into a shape like a wall. Suddenly, the paper becomes very stiff. It does not flop down anymore.

Steel works the exact same way.

To make the long walkway plank strong, the factory takes the flat steel sheet and bends the long side edges. This bent edge is called a rib. The number of times the steel is bent is called the number of folds.

The 2-Fold Plank Explained

In a 2-fold plank, the edge of the steel board is bent twice. First, it is bent straight down. Second, it is bent straight in. This creates a basic strong edge.

The standard rib height (how far down it bends) for a 2-fold plank is 38 mm. This size is very common. It is strong enough for normal building work. If you have painters, light cleaning, or standard brickwork, the 2-fold plank handles the weight easily without bending too much.

The 3-Fold Plank Explained

In a 3-fold plank, the edge of the steel board is bent three times. It bends straight down, straight in, and then it bends up one more time. This tiny extra fold makes a massive difference.

Because of this third fold, the edge becomes like a small steel box. It is incredibly stiff. 3-fold planks are usually made with a taller rib height. Instead of 38 mm, they can be 42 mm, 45 mm, or even 50 mm tall.

Why do we need a 3-fold plank?

  • Heavy Duty Work: If workers are carrying heavy stones, bricks, or large machines.
  • Longer Spans: If the supports for the board are very far apart (like 3000 mm), a 2-fold plank might bend too much. A 3-fold plank stays straight.
  • Longer Life: It takes more damage to break or bend a 3-fold plank.

Safety Standards Made Simple

Good companies like Prime Steeltech make sure their planks follow big safety rules. Here is what those letters and numbers mean:

  • IS 3696 & IS 4014: Indian safety rules for scaffolding. They say the board must not bend easily.
  • OSHA 29-1926: Safety rules from America. They protect workers from falling.
  • EN 12811-1: Safety rules from Europe.
  • EN ISO 1461: The rule for the zinc coating. It means the plank is protected from rust with at least 45 µm of zinc.

Whether you buy 2-fold or 3-fold, the plank must pass these tests!

How to Hook them Up

A strong plank is useless if it slips off the scaffold. Planks have parts at the end called "hooks" (often a Hook 50 type). These hooks grab onto the round scaffold pipes (the ledgers). The hooks are welded on. To stop rust, the factory paints the welded areas with a special aluminum-rich paint.

Safety Rule: The gap between two planks should never be more than 25 mm. If there is a big gap, a worker's foot could get stuck! Also, before using any plank, check the welds and hooks. If a hook is bent or the anti-slip dimples are worn out, throw the plank away. Never try to weld and fix it yourself on the site.

Conclusion

Choosing between a 2-fold and a 3-fold plank is simple. If you are doing standard work, a 2-fold plank with a 38 mm rib is perfect. It is safe, strong, and light.

If you are carrying heavy items, or your scaffold supports are spread far apart, spend a little extra and get a 3-fold plank with a taller rib (like 45 mm or 50 mm). That extra fold gives the steel superpowers to stay perfectly straight and keep your workers safe.

Remember: Height is dangerous, but good equipment makes it safe. Always check your planks, look for the IS or OSHA standard stamps, and make sure the dimples are clean so no one slips!
